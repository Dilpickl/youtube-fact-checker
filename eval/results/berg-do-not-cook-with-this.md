## [1:00] SUPPORTED: Industrial seed oil processing uses hexane, a solvent that is a component of gasoline.

> adding hexane which is a solvent that's in gasoline

Scientific reviews confirm that hexane is still the main solvent used to extract oil from seeds in industry, and the CDC's ATSDR says n-hexane comes from crude oil and is found in gasoline. One thing the claim leaves out: most of the hexane is removed during refining, so finished oils contain only trace amounts, if any.

- [Towards Substitution of Hexane as Extraction Solvent of Food Products and Ingredients with No Regrets](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9655691/): Says the food industry uses hexane widely to extract products such as vegetable oils, and looks at alternatives because of toxicity concerns.
- [Higher Yield and Polyphenol Content in Olive Pomace Extracts Using 2-Methyloxolane as Bio-Based Solvent](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9104984/): Says hexane is still the solvent of choice for extracting vegetable oils, even though it is toxic and harms the environment.

- [TotalEnergies – Solvents for Vegetable Oil Extraction](https://specialfluids.totalenergies.com/en/solvents-vegetable-oil-extraction): Sells a hexane-based hydrocarbon solvent made specifically for extracting vegetable oil.

## [1:30] MISLEADING: Americans get about 25 to 30% of their calories from seed oils.

> we consume like 25 to 30% of our calories with this right here

The 20–30% figure comes mostly from groups campaigning against seed oils, while peer-reviewed estimates find soybean oil, the main seed oil, supplies only about 7% of Americans' calories. Seed oil use really has soared over the past century, but the higher figures tend to rely on food supply data that count oil thrown away as waste, so the 25–30% range looks like an upper-end estimate rather than an established fact.

- [Soy Nutrition and Research - The Soyfoods Council](https://www.thesoyfoodscouncil.com/soyfoodsresearch): Soybean oil is the most commonly consumed oil in American diets, making up more than half of US vegetable oil use and a little over 7% of total calories.
- [What Are Seed Oils? Definitions & Facts - Seed Oil Free Alliance](https://www.seedoilfreecertified.com/seed-oils): This advocacy group estimates that Americans get 20–30% of their daily calories from highly refined seed oils. This is the likely source of figures like the one in the claim.
- [If we are what we eat, Americans are corn and soy - CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2007/HEALTH/diet.fitness/09/22/kd.gupta.column/): Cites an NIH doctor who said soybeans account for about 10 percent of calories in the U.S. That is far below 25–30%, although it counts soybeans only.
- [What Are Seed Oils and Their Benefits? - U.S. Soy](https://www.ussoy.org/allaboutseedoils): This industry group says seed oils supply polyunsaturated fats that fit within American Heart Association recommendations. It does not support the claim that they make up 25–30% of calories.

## [3:02] MISLEADING: The process for producing seed oils was developed around 1865, and the oils were not originally intended for human consumption.

> even like in 1865 when they kind of found a process to develop these seed oils it wasn't meant for human consumption

It's partly true: 19th-century cottonseed came from a waste stream, and its oil was first used mostly for soap, lamp fuel and other industrial products before refining by David Wesson and hydrogenation (which led to Crisco in 1911) turned it into a food. But no single seed-oil process was invented in 1865, mechanical oil presses go back to the early 1800s, and food use was always part of the cottonseed oil business, so saying the oil 'wasn't meant for human consumption' oversimplifies the history.

- [How Crisco Made Americans Believers in Industrial Food (Smithsonian)](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/how-crisco-made-americans-believers-industrial-food-180973845/): For most of the 19th century cotton seeds were a nuisance. Cottonseed oil only appealed to consumers after David Wesson developed bleaching and deodorizing in the late 1800s, and it was also linked to soap and industrial uses.
- [Criminal conspiracy to steal used cooking oil shows how trash can become treasure (The Conversation)](https://theconversation.com/criminal-conspiracy-to-steal-used-cooking-oil-shows-how-trash-can-become-treasure-292653): Cottonseed oil was a waste product. Makers crushed the seed for oil used in soap and cooking, and as a substitute for animal fats.
- [Seed Oils](https://everything-everywhere.com/seed-oils/): Mechanical screw presses for extracting seed oil were developed in the early 1800s, and cottonseed oil became the first widely used industrial seed oil in the U.S. Hydrogenation patents were first meant for making soap.

## [4:34] CONTRADICTED: Seed oils damage mitochondria and cause insulin resistance, leading to fatty liver, obesity, heart disease and increased cancer risk.

> this is why seed oils lead to insulin resistance

Large studies and reviews find that linoleic acid, the main fat in seed oils, is linked to a lower risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes, not higher insulin resistance. Major reviews also find no consistent evidence that seed oils damage mitochondria or cause fatty liver, obesity or cancer in humans.

- [Higher linoleic acid levels linked to lower heart disease and diabetes risk](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1084800): A study that measured linoleic acid in the blood linked higher levels to lower cardiometabolic risk, including heart disease and type 2 diabetes.
- [Health Implications of Linoleic Acid and Seed Oil Intake (Nutrition Today)](https://journals.lww.com/nutritiontodayonline/fulltext/2026/01000/health_implications_of_linoleic_acid_and_seed_oil.5.aspx): A review concluding that seed oils are a healthy part of the diet and should replace foods high in saturated fat, and that their processing follows safety limits.
- [Seed oil misinformation (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seed_oil_misinformation): A summary of the evidence saying seed oils are safe and beneficial, with omega-6 fats linked to lower cardiovascular risk and no consistent evidence that they increase inflammation.
- [More Evidence That Seed Oils Reduce Chance of Cardiac Disease](https://www.technologynetworks.com/applied-sciences/news/more-evidence-that-seeds-oils-arent-bad-for-us-400434): Reports new research suggesting linoleic acid may be good for heart health, against the popular online claim that seed oils are harmful.

## [5:05] MISLEADING: Seed oils are not burned as fuel in the body but are stored and accumulate in it.

> the seed oils are not being burned up as fuel in the body so they kind of get lodged and stored

Linoleic acid, the main fat in seed oils, does build up in body fat as people eat more of it: adipose levels in Americans roughly doubled over the past 50 years. But it is also burned for energy through normal beta-oxidation like other fatty acids, so saying seed oils are "not being burned up as fuel" is false and overstates the point about storage.

- [Guyenet & Carlson (2015), Increase in adipose tissue linoleic acid of US adults in the last half century, Advances in Nutrition](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26567191/): Linoleic acid in US adults' body fat rose by about 136% between 1959 and 2008, which tracked rising seed-oil intake. This supports the idea that it accumulates in body fat but does not show that it isn't burned.
- [DeLany et al. (2000), Differential oxidation of individual dietary fatty acids in humans, Am J Clin Nutr](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9497178/): Labeled-tracer studies in humans found that dietary linoleic acid is oxidized for energy. Polyunsaturated fats like linoleic acid were oxidized at rates similar to or higher than stearic acid, so seed-oil fats are used as fuel.

## [3:32] MISLEADING: Fatty acids from seed oils get incorporated into and replace components of cell membranes.

> the seed oils can actually get into the membranes and replace the membranes

It's well documented that fatty acids from food, including linoleic acid from seed oils, get built into cell membranes and change their fatty-acid makeup. But they replace some fatty acids inside the membrane, not the membrane itself, and this finding alone doesn't show the harm the video implies.

- [Increase in Adipose Tissue Linoleic Acid of US Adults in the Last Half Century (Advances in Nutrition)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S216183132300114X): A systematic review found that linoleic acid in US adults' fat tissue rose by about 136% over 50 years as diets shifted toward seed oils like soybean oil, and suggests linoleic acid may have harmful effects. This supports the idea that body tissues absorb dietary seed-oil fats, though the review measured fat tissue, not membranes directly.
- [Conjugated linoleic acid reduces permeability and fluidity of adipose plasma membranes from obese Zucker rats](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0006291X10011691): In obese rats, a linoleic-acid isomer from the diet (CLA) was built into membrane phospholipids and made the membranes stiffer. The paper notes these fatty acids are taken into membrane lipids quickly.
