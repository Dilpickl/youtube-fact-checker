## [10:47] SUPPORTED: About 8,200 years ago, a huge glacial lake in North America (Lake Agassiz), larger than today's Caspian Sea, drained suddenly into the ocean when its ice dam failed.

Sources agree that Lake Agassiz drained into Hudson Bay and the North Atlantic around 8,200 years ago, with a sudden burst after its ice dam gave way. The mechanism is debated (some studies favor subglacial drainage under a weakened ice saddle), and I did not find a source that explicitly compares the lake to the Caspian Sea, though its maximum size was far larger.

- [Lake Agassiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Agassiz): Says the last major drainage shift occurred around 8,200 years ago, with the lake draining into what is now Hudson Bay after the Hudson Bay ice melted.
- [Subglacial origin of Lake Agassiz-Ojibway final outburst flood](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/31969704_Subglacial_origin_of_Lake_Agassiz-Ojibway_final_outburst_flood): Says the lake drained catastrophically and subglacially beneath a grounded ice dam, with the Hudson Bay Ice Saddle collapse between about 8.57 and 8.11 thousand years ago.

## [11:20] SUPPORTED: The drainage of the glacial lake about 8,200 years ago raised global sea levels by 1 to 2 meters and slowed the Atlantic overturning circulation, cooling and drying the North Atlantic, Europe and Near East with a drought lasting over a century.

> Global sea levels rose by 1 to 2 m and the great conveyor belt that carried warm water northwards slowed down.

Published estimates of the sea-level rise from the final Lake Agassiz–Ojibway drainage range from about 0.8 to 2.2 m (Li et al. 2012), with Mississippi Delta estimates of 1.2 m and 1.5 ± 0.7 m, so the 1–2 m figure falls within the published range, though a 2021 study gives only 0.46 m from the lakes and up to 2.6 m total for the event. The lake-drainage slowdown of the Atlantic overturning circulation, with cooling and drying across the North Atlantic region, is the widely accepted mainstream explanation, though some modelling work (Matero et al. 2017) argues ice-saddle collapse played the larger role.

- [Synchronizing a sea-level jump, final Lake Agassiz drainage, and abrupt cooling 8200 years ago (Earth and Planetary Science Letters, 2012)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012E&PSL.315...41L/abstract): The total inferred eustatic sea-level rise from the very final stage of Lake Agassiz–Ojibway drainage at 8.2 ka ranges from 0.8 to 2.2 m.
- [New estimates of the magnitude of the sea-level jump during the 8.2 ka event (Geology)](https://pubs.geoscienceworld.org/gsa/geology/article/50/1/86/608090/New-estimates-of-the-magnitude-of-the-sea-level): Cites sea-level rise estimates of 1.2 m (Törnqvist et al. 2004) and 1.5 ± 0.7 m (Li et al. 2012) from the Mississippi Delta, and reports a larger total jump of 2.60 ± 0.88 m.
- [New estimates of the magnitude of the sea-level jump during the 8.2 ka event — FAQ summary](https://pubs.geoscienceworld.org/gsa/geology/article/50/1/86/608090/New-estimates-of-the-magnitude-of-the-sea-level): Estimates a global sea-level rise of 0.46 m from Lakes Agassiz and Ojibway drainage, with a further 0.44 m from the Laurentide Ice Sheet.
- [Hydroclimatic impacts of the abrupt cooling event 8200 years ago in the western Indo-Pacific Warm Pool](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01825-6): States it is widely accepted that drainage of the Laurentide proglacial lakes and ice sheets around 8.5 ka probably slowed the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, and that the 8.2 ka cold event communicated climate anomalies worldwide.
- [The 8.2 ka cooling event caused by Laurentide ice saddle collapse (Earth and Planetary Science Letters)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0012821X17303205): Argues the lake outburst played a minor role and the cooling could instead be caused by accelerated melt from the collapsing Hudson Bay ice saddle; cooling was 1–3 °C lasting about 160 years.
- [8.2-kiloyear event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8.2-kiloyear_event): Describes the event as engendering drier environmental conditions across much of the world and notes a 0.8 to 2.2 m sea-level range from Mississippi Delta data.
- [Abrupt weakening of the Indian summer monsoon at 8.2 kyr B.P.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0012821X14000363): Links an abrupt drying of Indian paleolake desiccation at about 8.2 ka to North Atlantic cooling associated with glacial outburst flood and AMOC slowdown.

## [14:40] SUPPORTED: Kushim, named on an Uruk clay tablet recording barley for beer production, is considered the first known personal name in recorded history.

> The first name in history, Kushim, a signature on a tablet recording Barney for beer

Kushim is widely regarded as the earliest known personal name in writing, appearing on Uruk-period tablets (c. 3300–3100 BCE) recording barley and beer production. Scholars note it could be a job title (an accountant or administrator) rather than a personal name, but the personal-name reading is the more common view.

- [First surviving personal name](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/first-surviving-personal-name): Calls Kushim the earliest individual whose name was recorded in a surviving document, an accountant or administrator in Uruk, and notes the title possibility but says the personal-name view is more commonly accepted.
- [Kushim (Uruk period)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushim_(Uruk_period)): Describes Kushim as potentially the earliest-known recorded name of a person in writing, found on several Uruk-period clay tablets recording barley transactions, and says it is uncertain whether the name refers to an individual or an office.
- ['World's First Signature'—an Early Biblical Name?](https://armstronginstitute.org/276-worlds-first-signature-an-early-biblical-name): Describes the tablet as recording 29,086 measures of barley over 37 months and as a beer-making reference, with the Kushim inscription believed to be the earliest example of a personal signature.
