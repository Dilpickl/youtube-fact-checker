## [1:00] SUPPORTED: Industrial seed oil processing uses hexane, a solvent that is a component of gasoline.

> adding hexane which is a solvent that's in gasoline

Hexane (mainly n-hexane) is the most common solvent used to extract oil from seeds like soybean, canola/rapeseed, and sunflower, and it is a minor component of gasoline, so the claim is accurate. The video's framing is alarming, but refining steps remove almost all residual hexane from the finished oil.

- [Hexane - an overview (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/hexane): Describes hexane as a colorless, volatile petroleum distillate present as a minor component of gasoline, and used as a solvent in extracting soybean, cottonseed, flaxseed and other oil seeds.
- [Is hexane in food a cause for concern? (EUFIC)](https://www.eufic.org/en/misinformation/article/is-hexane-in-food-a-cause-for-concern): Says hexane is a petroleum-derived solvent widely used to extract oils from seeds such as soybeans, rapeseed, and sunflower, and that refining steps remove almost all of it before the oil reaches shelves.
- [Method and composition for using organic, plant-derived, oil-extracted materials in gasoline additives (USPTO patent)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7160339): States that hexane extraction is the most commonly used technique for extracting oil from seeds.

## [1:30] MISLEADING: Americans get about 25 to 30% of their calories from seed oils.

> we consume like 25 to 30% of our calories with this right here

The 25–30% figure traces to advocacy estimates (e.g., Seed Oil Free Alliance's 20–30%, and a 24–32% figure from a secondary blog citing Knobbe) rather than measured intake. Measured data point lower: NHANES-based estimates put linoleic acid, the main polyunsaturated fat in seed oils, at roughly 7% of calories, and one source puts soybean oil at about 7% of American calories, so the claim overstates what the research shows.

- [Soybeans and the U.S. Food Supply - SNI Global](https://sniglobal.org/soybeans-and-the-u-s-food-supply/): Estimates that about 7% of calories consumed by Americans come from soybean oil, the most commonly consumed US cooking oil.
- [Trends in linoleic acid intake in the United States adult population: NHANES 1999–2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0952327818301108): Analyzes NHANES dietary data on linoleic acid, the primary polyunsaturated fat in the US diet, as a measured intake trend.

## [2:32] CONTRADICTED: Fast food restaurants reuse their frying oil around a hundred times.

> they don't just use this oil once they use it like a hundred times

Industry and test-based sources put reuse at roughly 3 to 10 uses before replacement, not about 100, and fast-food chains typically filter daily and replace oil every few days to a week. No source found supports a figure near 100 uses.

- [How Many Times Can You Reuse Frying Oil? - America's Test Kitchen](https://www.americastestkitchen.com/cooksillustrated/articles/1733-how-many-times-can-you-reuse-frying-oil): Tested oil for breaded chicken was fine through four uses, and the recommendation is three or four reuses for breaded foods, at least eight for cleaner-frying items like potato chips.
- [McDonald's Employees Say This Is How Often Frying Oil Gets Changed](https://www.yahoo.com/lifestyle/articles/mcdonalds-employees-often-frying-oil-151000782.html): Most McDonald's employees say fryer oil is changed every seven to 10 days, with filtering happening daily; industry guidelines recommend changing as often as every three to five days.
- [How Often Does a Restaurant Change Fryer Oil? - RS*](https://restaurantsuperstar.com/how-often-does-a-restaurant-change-fryer-oil/): States that fryer oil must be replenished after roughly 8 to 10 uses, and that some fast-food restaurants replace oil and clean fryers twice a week.
- [Can You Reuse Frying Oil? - WebstaurantStore](https://www.webstaurantstore.com/blog/5336/can-you-reuse-frying-oil.html): Says most restaurants reuse frying oil 3–5 times, depending on oil type and conditions.
- [How Often Should Commercial Fryer Oil Be Changed? - Commercial Equipment Service Inc.](https://commercialequipmentserviceinc.com/2022/07/how-often-should-commercial-fryer-oil-be-changed/): Recommends changing or filtering oil every three to four times for non-breaded poultry or meat, and every six to eight times for fries or non-breaded foods; high-volume fast food should change oil weekly.

## [3:02] MISLEADING: The process for producing seed oils was developed around 1865, and the oils were not originally intended for human consumption.

> even like in 1865 when they kind of found a process to develop these seed oils it wasn't meant for human consumption

Cottonseed oil, the first major industrial seed oil, was indeed a waste byproduct first used for lamp fuel, soap and lubricants rather than food. But the sources place its commercial start in the 1830s to 1850s, not a single 1865 process, and the 1865 date itself isn't supported by anything I found.

- [Seed Oils](https://everything-everywhere.com/seed-oils/): Says cottonseed oil was the first widely used industrial seed oil in the U.S. and was not used for human consumption at first because of its bitter taste and impurities.
- [A Popular American Food Ingredient Was Machine Lubricant First - MotorBiscuit](https://www.motorbiscuit.com/popular-food-oil-machine-lubricant/): Reports the first commercial cottonseed oil was marketed as lamp fuel and machine grease, with the first cottonseed oil mill built in the 1830s.
- [The Shocking Origin of Vegetable Oil — Garbage!](https://fung.substack.com/p/the-shocking-origin-of-vegetable): Says commercial cottonseed oil production could begin in the 1850s once the technology matured, and that demand collapsed after the 1859 oil drilling boom.
- [The Rise and Fall Of Seed Oils - by Roger Erickson](https://econcurrents.substack.com/p/the-rise-and-fall-of-seed-oils): Says alkali refining made cottonseed oil edible by the 1850s and that it was initially used in soaps and lamp oil before being marketed as a cheap lard substitute.

## [4:03] CONTRADICTED: The oxidative stress from smoking persists for about 6 months to a year after quitting.

> the oxidative stress that occurs with smoking kind of poisons us for about 6 months to a year after quitting

A 2026 Cureus systematic review of 10 studies (2,750 participants) found oxidative stress markers improve within one to two weeks of quitting, earlier than inflammatory and lipid markers. Some airway studies show oxidant burden lingering for about three months, but no source found supports a 6-month-to-1-year persistence.

- [Physiologic Recovery After Smoking Cessation: A Systematic Review of Inflammatory and Cardiovascular Biomarker Changes (Cureus)](https://www.cureus.com/articles/500877-physiologic-recovery-after-smoking-cessation-a-systematic-review-of-inflammatory-and-cardiovascular-biomarker-changes): Oxidative stress markers improve within one to two weeks of abstinence, while inflammatory markers decline over about two to 12 weeks and lipid and hematologic parameters over three to eight months.
- [Physiologic Recovery After Smoking Cessation: A Systematic Review of Inflammatory and Cardiovascular Biomarker Changes - PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13456119/): Oxidative stress is described as improving earliest in the sequence of biomarker normalization after cessation, with a significant decrease in the marker 8-epi-PGF2α after 1-2 weeks.
- [Persistence of oxidant and protease burden in the airways after smoking cessation](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1471-2466-9-25): In this study of airway oxidant markers, some decline occurred over three months after quitting, though the oxidant and protease load was still seen months after cessation.
- [Reduction in oxidatively generated DNA damage following smoking cessation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3104490): Oxidatively generated DNA damage decreased after quitting, with smoking cessation possibly reducing oxidative damage by 30-50 percent in the short term (11 weeks).
- [Persistence of oxidant and protease burden in the airways after smoking cessation (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2697135/): Ex-smokers who quit more than 20 years ago had oxidative stress marker levels similar to never smokers, suggesting oxidative stress declines over time after quitting.

## [4:03] CONTRADICTED: A single serving of seed oil has toxic effects in the body for about three years.

> it's going to poison us for about 3 years after just one serving

The roughly 680-day half-life of linoleic acid in fat tissue is real, but it describes how long fat stores take to turn over after long-term intake, not a three-year toxic effect from one serving. Reviewers found no human evidence that seed oils cause harm, and the toxicity concerns come mainly from heating and from animal or cell studies.

- [Narrative review in Nutrients (2023), cited via secondary coverage](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10386285/): Summarized in secondary coverage as giving a linoleic acid adipose half-life of about 680 days; the review is about accumulated stores, not single-serving toxicity.
- [Seed Oils: Frying Up Controversy (ACS ChemMatters)](https://www.acs.org/education/chemmatters/articles/seed-oils-frying-up-controversy.html): Quotes a researcher saying clinical trials giving people seed oils show no signs of harm, and long-term consumers show no negative signals.
- [Are Seed Oils Toxic? - UNH Extension](https://extension.unh.edu/blog/2025/08/are-seed-oils-toxic): States scientific evidence does not fully support claims that seed oils are toxic, and notes concerns about heated-oil compounds are mostly from animal or cell studies, not human trials.
- [Do Seed Oils Make You Sick? - Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/health/healthy-eating/do-seed-oils-make-you-sick-a1363483895/): Reports that claims about seed oils containing toxic byproducts from processing are not well supported by the evidence reviewed.

## [4:34] CONTRADICTED: Seed oils damage mitochondria and cause insulin resistance, leading to fatty liver, obesity, heart disease and increased cancer risk.

> this is why seed oils lead to insulin resistance

The claim states as established fact that seed oils cause insulin resistance, fatty liver, heart disease and cancer, but the human evidence points the other way: a feeding trial and meta-analysis found PUFA-rich diets improved insulin resistance and liver fat, and prospective cohort meta-analyses link higher linoleic acid intake to lower heart disease and cancer mortality. The mitochondria-based 'energy model' is a published hypothesis supported mainly by animal and mechanistic work, and critics argue it lacks direct clinical support.

- [Seed Oils on Trial: Is the Panic Justified? - Sigma Nutrition](https://sigmanutrition.com/seedoils/): Summarizes controlled trials and a feeding-trial meta-analysis reporting that PUFA intake improved glycaemia and insulin resistance, and that saturated fat increased liver fat more than PUFA.
- [Dietary Linoleic Acid and Risk of Coronary Heart Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Prospective Cohort Studies](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/circulationaha.114.010236): Concludes that dietary linoleic acid intake is inversely associated with coronary heart disease risk in a dose-response manner, and finds little evidence that higher intake raises cancer risk.
- [Dietary intake and biomarkers of linoleic acid and mortality: systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies - The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(22)00778-X/fulltext): Reports that higher linoleic acid intake was associated with modestly lower risk of all-cause, CVD and cancer mortality in prospective cohort studies.
- [Linoleic acid intake and cancer risk: a review and meta-analysis - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9665108/): Found that no combined estimates from within-population studies indicated a significantly increased cancer risk with high versus low linoleic acid or polyunsaturated fat intake.
- [Commentary: The energy model of insulin resistance: a unifying theory linking seed oils to metabolic disease and cancer](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12362986/): A critical commentary arguing the energy model's central thesis is not supported by direct experimental or clinical evidence, and citing a systematic review finding seed oils improve insulin sensitivity.
- [The energy model of insulin resistance: A unifying theory linking seed oils to metabolic disease and cancer - PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12105547/): Presents the energy model as a hypothesis that seed oils harm mitochondria and drive insulin resistance, relying largely on animal studies and mechanistic evidence.
- [Linoleic Acid: A Narrative Review of the Effects of Increased Intake in the Standard American Diet and Associations with Chronic Disease - PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10386285/): A narrative review that proposes excess linoleic acid may impair mitochondria and contribute to chronic disease, citing mostly mechanistic and animal evidence, which represents the minority view.

## [5:05] CONTRADICTED: Seed oils are not burned as fuel in the body but are stored and accumulate in it.

> the seed oils are not being burned up as fuel in the body so they kind of get lodged and stored

Seed oils (linoleic acid-rich) are in fact oxidized for energy: a study in obese men found all dietary linoleate was beta-oxidized, with linoleate beta-oxidation exceeding intake 2.5-fold during weight loss. Some linoleic acid does accumulate in adipose tissue (adipose LA tracks dietary intake, with a half-life of about 680 days), but accumulation is not a substitute for being burned, so the claim that they are 'not burned as fuel' is false.

- [Beta-oxidation of linoleate in obese men undergoing weight loss - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11273844/): Concludes that all dietary linoleate is beta-oxidized and that at least an equivalent amount is lost from the body during moderate weight loss.
- [Increase in Adipose Tissue Linoleic Acid of US Adults in the Last Half Century - PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4642429/): Adipose tissue linoleic acid correlates strongly with dietary LA intake (R² = 0.81), and LA incorporation into adipose tissue has a half-life of about 680 days.
- [Increase in Adipose Tissue Linoleic Acid of US Adults in the Last Half Century - Advances in Nutrition](https://advances.nutrition.org/article/S2161-8313(23)00114-X/fulltext): Adipose tissue LA is used as a biomarker of dietary intake, showing that dietary LA is stored in fat tissue over time.
- [Linoleate-Rich Safflower Oil Diet Increases Linoleate-Derived Bioactive Lipid Mediators in Plasma, and Brown and White Adipose Depots of Healthy Mice](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9412644/): States that polyunsaturated fats are energy substrates as well as precursors to lipid mediators, and that adipose tissue provides energy storage.

## [5:36] MISLEADING: Among cooking oils, soybean oil produces the most peroxidation, followed by corn, sunflower and canola, with olive oil, beef tallow and coconut oil producing less.

## [3:32] SUPPORTED: Fatty acids from seed oils get incorporated into and replace components of cell membranes.

> the seed oils can actually get into the membranes and replace the membranes

Peer-reviewed research consistently shows dietary linoleic acid (the main fatty acid in seed oils) is incorporated into cell membrane phospholipids, and membrane fatty acid composition tracks dietary intake. The video's wording that seed oils "replace the membranes" is an exaggeration, since fatty acids swap into existing membrane lipids rather than replacing whole membranes.

- [Dietary linoleic acid and human health: Focus on cardiovascular and cardiometabolic effects](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021915019315758): Within cells, LA can be incorporated into cell membrane lipids or used as a substrate for metabolic reactions, and in cell membranes it is incorporated in the sn-2 position of phospholipids.
- [Dietary linoleic acid modulates liver plasma membrane unsaturated fatty acid composition, phosphatidylcholine and cholesterol content](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0271531785801433): An increase in dietary linoleic acid increased the linoleic acid content of phosphatidylcholine in liver plasma membranes.
- [Effects of increasing dietary linoleic acid on phospholipid fatty acid composition in Atlantic salmon](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1589446/): Incorporation of linoleic acid into membrane phospholipids of leucocytes and gills increased in response to dietary intake.
- [Linoleic Acid - an overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/linoleic-acid): The type of fatty acids that predominate in a cell membrane differs among cell types and is also influenced by the fatty acid composition of the diet.
