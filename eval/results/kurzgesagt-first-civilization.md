## [1:28] SUPPORTED: The Holocene, the current geological epoch, began about 12,000 years ago at the end of the last ice age.

> This is the start of the holysine, our current geological period and the end of the last ice age

Geologists formally date the start of the Holocene to about 11,700 years ago, so "about 12,000 years ago" is a fair rounding, and the Holocene began as the last glacial period ended. The Holocene is technically an epoch within the Quaternary Period rather than a "period", but in everyday speech that's a harmless slip.

- [Holocene — Research News & Scientific Publications (Phys.org)](https://phys.org/concepts/holocene/): Describes the Holocene as the current geological epoch within the Quaternary Period, beginning about 11,700 years before present, after the Pleistocene and the last major glaciation ended.
- [Last Glacial Period (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Period): Says the last glacial period ran from about 115,000 to 11,700 years ago and ended where the Holocene begins.
- [Holocene (Simple English Wikipedia)](https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene): Says the Holocene marks the end of the Pleistocene ice ages, with temperatures rising quickly about 11,700 years ago.

## [3:30] SUPPORTED: Monumental construction at Karahan Tepe in southern Anatolia began about 12,000 years ago, roughly 7,000 years before the Egyptian pyramids.

> They're celebrating the first great construction of humanity 7,000 years before the pyramids.

Karahan Tepe dates to roughly 10,000–9,500 BC, about 11,500–12,000 years ago, so "about 12,000 years ago" is a fair round figure. The first Egyptian pyramids went up around 2600 BC, roughly 7,000 years later. One caveat: the excavators place the site's start slightly later than the top of that range, closer to 11,000–11,400 years ago.

## [7:58] SUPPORTED: At Çatalhöyük, people buried their dead beneath house floors and later exhumed some to remove and decorate the skulls, including with plaster.

> After a few years, their bodies were exumed and their heads removed, decorated, and painted.

Excavations confirm that people at Çatalhöyük buried their dead beneath house floors and platforms, and that skulls were sometimes taken from these burials after they were first interred. Skull decoration happened but was rare: so far only one plastered and painted skull has been found at the site, so it was not routine.

- [Skull Retrieval and Secondary Burial Practices in the Neolithic Near East: Recent Insights from Çatalhöyük, Turkey](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318042962_Skull_Retrieval_and_Secondary_Burial_Practices_in_the_Neolithic_Near_East_Recent_Insights_from_Catalhoyuk_Turkey): Describes burials beneath house floors at Çatalhöyük, including a case where stratigraphy confirms a skull was removed after the body was first buried.
- [Çatalhöyük (Smarthistory)](https://smarthistory.org/catalhoyuk/): Says the dead were placed under floors or platforms in houses and that skulls were sometimes removed and plastered.
- [Çatal Newsletter 11 (Çatalhöyük Research Project)](https://www.catalhoyuk.com/newsletters/11/nl11_01.html): Reports the first painted and plastered human skull found at Çatalhöyük, in Building 42, held in a buried woman's arms.

## [10:47] SUPPORTED: About 8,200 years ago, a huge glacial lake in North America (Lake Agassiz), larger than today's Caspian Sea, drained suddenly into the ocean when its ice dam failed.

> Around the year 3,800 of the human era, a sudden catastrophe happened in North America.

The research supports this. The combined Lake Agassiz–Ojibway drained catastrophically into the North Atlantic through Hudson Bay when the Laurentide ice dam broke, at about the time of the 8.2-kiloyear cooling event (the year 3,800 on the Human Era calendar is about 6200 BC, or roughly 8,200 years ago). At its peak, Agassiz is generally estimated at about 440,000 km², bigger than the Caspian's roughly 371,000 km². Two caveats: some studies date the flood a little earlier, around 8,400–8,200 years ago, and some describe it as two drainage pulses or partly a flood under the ice rather than one dam failure.

- [Paleohydraulics of the last outburst flood from glacial Lake Agassiz and the 8200BP cold event (Quaternary Science Reviews)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0277379103002865): Describes huge lakes that formed at the edge of the Laurentide Ice Sheet. When the ice dam across Hudson Bay was breached, Lake Agassiz released a massive outburst flood, which the paper links to the 8200 BP cold event.
- [8.2-kiloyear event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8.2-kiloyear_event): A rapid global cooling about 8,200 years ago that lasted 2–4 centuries. It is widely attributed to the drainage of Lake Agassiz–Ojibway into the North Atlantic.
- [Lake Ojibway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Ojibway): The lake was dammed by the Laurentide Ice Sheet. Recent studies say it drained in two separate events, through a mix of ice-dam breach and flooding beneath the ice.
- [Connecting the Final Drainage of Lake Agassiz to the 8.2 ka Event (Mississippi Delta sea-level record)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/253397952_Connecting_the_Final_Drainage_of_Lake_Agassiz_to_the_82_ka_Event_by_Means_of_a_High_Resolution_Sea-Level_Record_From_the_Mississippi_Delta): Says the catastrophic early-Holocene drainage of Lakes Agassiz and Ojibway is widely believed to have triggered the 8.2 ka cold event, and uses sea-level data to tie the two together.

## [11:20] SUPPORTED: The drainage of the glacial lake about 8,200 years ago raised global sea levels by 1 to 2 meters and slowed the Atlantic overturning circulation, cooling and drying the North Atlantic, Europe and Near East with a drought lasting over a century.

> Global sea levels rose by 1 to 2 m and the great conveyor belt that carried warm water northwards slowed down.

Sea-level records from around the world show a sudden rise of roughly 1 to 3 m around this time, tied to the final drainage of Lake Agassiz–Ojibway, so 1–2 m falls within the published estimates (0.8–2.2 m from the Mississippi Delta, about 2.1 m locally in the Rhine delta, and some higher). It is widely accepted that the freshwater slowed the Atlantic overturning circulation, causing cold, dry conditions across the Northern Hemisphere, including the Near East, for about 160 years. One caveat: the lake water alone may explain only about 0.4–1.4 m, and part of the jump may have come from collapsing ice in the Hudson Bay area or from Antarctica.

- [Synchronizing a sea-level jump, final Lake Agassiz drainage, and abrupt cooling 8200 years ago (Li et al., EPSL)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0012821X11003177): A Mississippi Delta record shows a sea-level jump of 0.8–2.2 m at 8.18–8.31 ka, at the same time as the 8.2 ka cooling, most likely caused by the final drainage of Lake Agassiz–Ojibway.
- [Timing and magnitude of the sea-level jump preluding the 8200 yr event (Hijma & Cohen 2010, Geology)](https://pubs.geoscienceworld.org/gsa/geology/article-abstract/38/3/275/130243/Timing-and-magnitude-of-the-sea-level-jump): Measured a local jump of about 2.1 ± 0.9 m in the Rhine delta and inferred a global average of 3.0 ± 1.2 m, larger than earlier estimates of 0.4–1.4 m.
- [New estimates of the magnitude of the sea-level jump during the 8.2 ka event (Obrist-Farner et al. 2021, Geology)](https://pubs.geoscienceworld.org/gsa/geology/article/50/1/86/608090/New-estimates-of-the-magnitude-of-the-sea-level): Found a relative sea-level jump of 2.60 ± 0.88 m in Guatemala and says the lake drainage alone cannot fully explain it.
- [Evidence for the impact of the 8.2-kyBP climate event on Near Eastern early farmers (PNAS)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1803607115): Proxy records worldwide show generally dry and cold conditions lasting about 160 years, including evidence from the Near East.
- [8.2-kiloyear event (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8.2-kiloyear_event): Describes rapid global cooling about 8,200 years ago lasting two to four centuries, likely caused by meltwater from Lake Agassiz disrupting the Atlantic overturning circulation.

## [14:40] MISLEADING: Kushim, named on an Uruk clay tablet recording barley for beer production, is considered the first known personal name in recorded history.

> The first name in history, Kushim, a signature on a tablet recording Barney for beer

Kushim does appear on Uruk-period tablets (c. 3200 BC) recording barley, and popular sources such as Harari's Sapiens call it the oldest known name. But scholars aren't sure whether Kushim was a person, a job title or an institution, so 'potentially the earliest' is more accurate than calling it definitively 'the first name in history.'

- [Kushim (Uruk period) – Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushim_(Uruk_period)): Calls Kushim 'potentially the earliest-known recorded name of a person' on Uruk-period barley tablets such as MS 1717, but says it is uncertain whether the word refers to an individual, an officeholder's title or an institution.
- [The Oldest Name in The World – Amusing Planet](https://www.amusingplanet.com/2022/04/the-oldest-name-in-world.html): Describes the barley tablet and says the sounds of the two signs are known but not what they mean together. The name reading rests partly on where the signs sit at the end of the inscription.
- [The Oldest Known Name In Human History – TheTravel](https://www.thetravel.com/what-is-the-oldest-name-in-history/): Presents Kushim, an accountant, as the world's first recorded name. This reflects the common popular framing.
